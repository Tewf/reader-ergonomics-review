# Review: five strands

Step 2 of the review, read on 2026-11-03. It groups the 25 sources by strand and answers Q1 to Q4 of [frame](frame.md). Surveys first in each strand.

- Each key is in the [bibliography](../bibliography.md).
- Grades are the standard's four.
- A figure read in an abstract is marked (secondary).

## Strand 0: existing reviews

- A tertiary study found only 12 secondary studies that used grey literature, from 2009 to 2019. They followed guidelines for reviews that give no advice on quality analysis and synthesis of grey sources (Neto 2019, abstract, secondary). Measured, descriptive.
- A second tertiary study took nine multivocal reviews of 2017 to 2019. Grey sources gave evidence absent from the formal literature, and 19 types of grey source were used (Kamei 2021, abstract, secondary). Measured, descriptive.
- Of 138 reviews in software engineering, 105 (76.09%) included grey literature, and 582 of 6,307 primary studies (9.23%) were grey (Yasin 2020, abstract, secondary). The authors propose their own quality checklist for grey sources. Measured, descriptive.
- Garousi 2019 takes the guidelines of Kitchenham and Charters as its baseline (abstract, read). Kitchenham 2009 is the review of reviews it builds on; it was located and not read.
- No study of how the files of a review are kept was found. No program that checks a review's report was found (protocol, F13 to F15).

## Strand 1: guidelines and reporting (Q1)

- PRISMA 2020 is a checklist of 27 items with an abstract checklist of 12 items and two flow diagrams (Page 2021, Tables 1 and 2, read). Convention, built by consensus.
- Item 7 asks for the full search strategy of every source; item 8, how many reviewers screened each record and whether they worked independently (Page 2021, Table 1, read).
- Item 16a asks for the flow from records found to studies included; item 16b, the studies excluded with the reason (Page 2021, Table 1, read).
- Item 24 asks for registration and protocol, or a statement that there is none. Items 25 and 26 ask for support and competing interests; item 27, for which data and code are public (Page 2021, Table 1, read).
- Items 14 and 21 ask for an assessment of bias from missing results; items 15 and 22, for the certainty of the evidence (Page 2021, Table 1, read).
- PRISMA-S has 16 items for reporting a search, built in a three-stage Delphi process (Rethlefsen 2021, abstract, secondary). Convention.
- Garousi 2019 gives 14 guidelines. They rest on a survey of 24 guideline papers in other fields and the authors' own multivocal reviews (section 3, read). Convention.
- Guideline 2 asks that existing reviews be found first; guideline 8, that a stopping criterion be named: saturation, effort bounded, or evidence exhaustion (Garousi 2019, guidelines 2 and 8, read).
- Table 4 gives seven yes/no questions to decide on grey sources; one yes suggests including them (Garousi 2019, Table 4, read).
- The worksheet for grey sources that Garousi 2019 cites has a field for the number of hits (section 5.4.2, read).
- Kuhrmann 2017 offers an experience-based guideline for the data collection and selection steps (abstract, read). Convention.
- A standard-setting body marks searching grey literature as highly desirable, and asks that search strategies be peer reviewed before they are run (Lefebvre 2025, sections 4.3 and 4.4.8, read). Practice.

## Strand 2: grey sources (Q2)

- Garousi 2019 grades a grey source on 20 criteria in eight groups: authority 4, methodology 6, objectivity 4, date 1, position 1, novelty 2, impact 1, outlet type 1 (Table 7, read). Outlet type is 1, 0.5 or 0 for tier 1, 2 or 3.
- A threshold of 10 of 20 appears as what the authors "could" set, applied to five example sources that scored 13, 19, 16, 15.5 and 12 (Garousi 2019, section 5.2, read). It is an illustration, not a rule. Convention.
- Garousi 2019 says agreement between persons can be measured on a three-point scale, and that disagreements must be discussed and resolved, for example by voting (section 5.2, read). It gives no agreement statistic.
- In nine multivocal reviews in software engineering from 2015 to 2018, the pool ran from 7 to 234 sources and the grey share from 14.3% to 85.9% (Garousi 2019, Table 2, read). Descriptive.
- In five studies of health trials, published trials showed a 9% greater effect than grey trials (ratio of odds ratios 1.09, 95% interval 1.03 to 1.16) (Hopewell 2007, abstract, secondary). Measured. Grey sources have to be found to limit bias.
- Grey literature can reduce publication bias and carry null results (Páez 2017, abstract, secondary). Supported with caveat.
- A review of 140 systematic reviews asked why grey sources are included or left out in management studies (Adams 2017, abstract, secondary). Descriptive.
- No study of how reliably two persons score a grey source was found (protocol, F6, F7, F9). Not found, in these searches.

## Strand 3: search and snowballing

- Of 495 sources of one review, 30% came from the protocol's database and hand searches, 51% from snowballing and 24% from personal knowledge or contacts (Greenhalgh 2005, abstract, secondary). Measured, descriptive; one review.
- Snowballing found 83% of the papers and database search 46%; the few it missed could have been found with a larger start set (Badampudi 2015, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- Snowballing and a database search found comparable papers, snowballing more efficiently in one case (Wohlin 2016, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- A hybrid search found 30% more primary studies than the database-led review it replicated (Wohlin 2022, abstract, secondary). Measured; one replication.
- Wohlin 2014 gives the guideline for snowballing and a replication (abstract, read in part). Jalali 2012 and Zhang 2011 were located and not read.

## Strand 4: the second reader (Q3)

- Of 22,571 records, 301 were flagged by at least one reviewer. Kappa was above 0.8 in two pairs, above 0.6 in three and above 0.4 in one (Edwards 2002, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- A single reviewer missed on average 8% of eligible reports (range 0 to 24%); pairs missed 0 to 1%. Two reviewers raised the trials found by 9% on average (Edwards 2002, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- Four evaluations covering 23 single screenings found a median of 5% of studies missed (range 0 to 58%): 3% for six experienced reviewers, 13% for three less experienced (Waffenschmidt 2019, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- In three of seven single screenings, all by one less experienced reviewer, the findings of the meta-analysis would have changed substantially (Waffenschmidt 2019, abstract, secondary). Single screening is not equivalent to double.
- A body that sets standards asks for at least two people working independently on eligibility. It allows one person to screen titles and abstracts, and it does not ask for searches in duplicate (Lefebvre 2025, page, read). Practice.
- Kappa corrects percent agreement for chance. The reading that 0.41 is acceptable may be too lenient for health research (McHugh 2012, abstract, secondary). Supported with caveat.
- A language model as screener matched humans in some tasks, but chance agreement and unbalanced data skewed its scores. Adjusted, its screening ranged from none to moderate, and its data extraction was moderate (Khraisha 2024, abstract, secondary). Measured, preregistered.
- Every source here concerns screening or extraction. None concerns the second rating of a quality score.

## Strand 5: checks

- A tool to assess adherence of reviews to PRISMA 2020, with a manual and an automated version, is in development (PRISMA Executive 2026, the PRISMA-Check page, read). Practice, not a released program.
- The searches found no program for the reporting of software engineering reviews (protocol, F13 to F15).
- Search reproducibility is a reporting item, not a program: item 7 asks that strategies be presented in full (Page 2021, Table 1, read).
